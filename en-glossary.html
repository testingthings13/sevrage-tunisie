The words you will hear.

Medical vocabulary is one more barrier, at the very moment you need one least. Here is what the words mean.

Addiction medicine
The medical discipline that treats dependences, with or without a substance.
Craving
The irrepressible urge to use. It comes in waves, triggered by a place, a person, a time of day or an emotion. It persists long after detoxification, and it is what the therapeutic work targets. See: what the body does when the substance stops.
Delirium tremens
A serious complication of alcohol withdrawal: confusion, hallucinations, intense tremors, fever, dehydration. It usually occurs between 48 and 96 hours after stopping. It is a medical emergency. See: alcohol withdrawal.
Dependence
A state in which stopping causes physical or psychological symptoms. Different from harmful use, where consumption causes damage without a withdrawal syndrome.
Detoxification
The phase in which the substance is eliminated. It lasts a few days. It is the short part of the treatment, often mistaken for the whole of it.
Day hospital
The patient comes in the morning and goes home to sleep. Workshops, sessions and treatments through the day, with no break from home or work. See: psychiatric care at the clinic.
Rehab
The everyday word for the whole journey: detox first — the short part — then the stay and the therapeutic work that make stopping last.
Relapse
A return to use after a period of abstinence. It is part of the history of most dependences; what matters is how quickly you come back to consult.
Withdrawal
Stopping the substance, and all the symptoms that come with it. “Medical withdrawal” means those symptoms are treated and monitored, not merely endured. See: the withdrawal protocol, from the first day to the last.
Substitution
Replacing an opioid with a medicine from the same family, long-acting and at a controlled dose (methadone, buprenorphine), to remove the withdrawal without the euphoria.
Withdrawal syndrome
The set of disturbances that appear on stopping: tremors, sweats, anxiety, insomnia, nausea. Its intensity depends on the substance, the doses and how long the use has lasted.
Mood stabiliser
A medicine that stabilises mood, used in particular in bipolar disorder.
CBT — cognitive behavioural therapy
A psychotherapy that identifies the thoughts and situations that precede use, then builds another response, prepared in advance.
Motivational interviewing
An approach that draws out the patient's own reasons rather than opposing them with the caregiver's. It is the one that obtains the most engagement in addiction medicine.
Dual diagnosis
The combination of an addiction and a psychiatric disorder. Treating one without the other exposes the patient to relapse.
